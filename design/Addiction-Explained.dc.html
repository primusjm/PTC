What addiction is

A chronic, treatable medical condition.

Classified as a chronic brain disorder by the American Society of Addiction Medicine and the National Institute on Drug Abuse.

Addiction is compulsive substance use or behaviour that continues despite harmful consequences. It takes hold when repeated engagement with a drug or an activity alters the brain's reward and self-control circuits, producing intense cravings and a real loss of control.

Tolerance builds with repeated misuse: larger amounts, or more frequent use, are needed for the same effect — which escalates the addiction on its own. Long-term use then becomes a cycle in which the substance is needed simply to avoid withdrawal, until it is prioritised above everything that once mattered.

The four categories of addictive behaviour identified in the DSM-5.

Two categories

Substances, and behaviours.

Both work on the same reward circuitry, and both can dominate a life. We treat substance use disorders here; where a behavioural addiction is present alongside one, it is addressed in therapy.

Category one

Substance use disorders

Compulsive use of chemical substances despite the damage to physical health, mental wellbeing and relationships.

Behavioural addictions

Dependency vs addiction

Not the same thing — and the difference matters.

A person can be dependent and never develop an addiction. A person can be addicted without being dependent on a substance. But once dependence sets in, the risk of addiction rises dramatically.

The distinction is control. Tolerance and withdrawal appear in both. In addiction, control over the substance is lost completely and it takes priority over everything else in the person's life.

Causes

There is no single cause. There is a combination.

Genetic, environmental and developmental factors together make some people far more vulnerable to the brain-altering effects of substances. None of them is a moral failing.
